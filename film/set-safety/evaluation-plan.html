How We'll Know the Safety Orientation Worked

A four-level evaluation plan for Last Light, using things a small production already has.

Owner: 1st AD (Chris Bell) Reports to: producer (Sam Ortiz) Covers: the Set Safety Orientation, taken by everyone before day 1 Shoot: 18 days

Purpose

The orientation exists to change what people do on set: cover cable, bag stands, foot ladders, keep exits clear, call holds and report near misses. Knowing the rules is necessary but not the goal. This plan checks all four levels, but it is built around Level 3, behaviour on set, because that's where the training either works or doesn't.

Level 1Reaction

Did people find it useful?

Instrument
Three questions at the end of the course, each on a 1–5 scale:
  1. "This was relevant to the work I'll be doing on this show."
  2. "The rules were clear enough to follow on set."
  3. "If I saw something unsafe, I would call a hold."
Target
Average 4 or higher on question 3. It's the reaction that matters most, because it predicts whether people will speak up.
Acting on it
Low scores on question 3 go into the day 1 safety meeting: the 1st AD says plainly that holds are welcome and thanks the first person who calls one.
Level 2Learning

Do people know the rules?

Instruments
The 8-question knowledge check (pass mark 7), and the hazard hunt: how many of the eight hazards each person finds before using "Show me".
Target
Everyone passes before their first call. Average hunt score of 6 or more.
Acting on it
Any question more than a quarter of people miss is rewritten, or its lesson is. The hazard most often missed becomes the first thing the 1st AD points out on the day 1 set walk.
Level 3Behaviour

Do people do it on set?

Instrument
A 10-minute set walk by the 1st AD with the key PA, at call and after each company move, using the checklist below. Plus a tally of holds called for safety, kept on the production report.
When
Days 1, 3 and 10. Day 1 is the baseline, day 3 shows whether it took, and day 10 shows whether it lasted.
Target
90% of checklist items met by day 3, and still met on day 10.

Why behaviour comes firstA crew can pass the knowledge check and still run cable across a doorway at 6 a.m. The set walk is the only measure here that shows the training working where it matters.
Level 4Results

Is the set safer?

Measures
Injuries from the incident log, split into first-aid-only and lost-time.
Near misses reported per week.
Time lost to safety incidents, from the production report.
Target
Zero lost-time injuries. Near-miss reports rise in week 1, then fall.
Reading it
A training that works makes people report more at first, not less. A low near-miss count in week 1 would mean people aren't reporting, not that the set is safe. The fall afterwards is the sign that hazards are being fixed.

Limits, and what it costs

  • No control group. One show can't compare trained and untrained crews. If the production company kept an incident log on its last show, compare against that, and check whether the behaviours seen on set are the ones the course taught.
  • Small numbers. With a crew of about 40, one incident moves the numbers a lot. Read Level 4 as a story, not a rate.
  • Cost. Nothing new to buy: the course, a printed checklist, the incident log and the production report. About 10 minutes of the 1st AD's time on three days, and a one-page weekly report.
